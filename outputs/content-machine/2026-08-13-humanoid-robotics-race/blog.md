# The Humanoid Robotics Race Nobody's Reading Right

Three companies are chasing the same prize right now: build a robot that can work alongside people in a factory, a warehouse, eventually a home. All three get covered as if they're running the same race. They're not. Figure AI, Tesla, and Unitree are making three fundamentally different bets, and only one of them is actually making money doing it.

That gap between funding, hype, and results is worth understanding, because it's not really a robotics story. It's a lesson in how to evaluate any AI vendor, including the ones knocking on your business's door right now.

## The Bull Case Nobody Questions: Figure AI's $39 Billion

Figure AI is the best-funded pure-play humanoid robotics company on the planet. $1.9 billion raised, a $39 billion post-money valuation after its latest round. Founder Brett Adcock built the company to own the whole stack: the robot itself (Figure 03), the AI model that drives it (Helix, built in-house after the company walked away from its OpenAI partnership), and the factory that manufactures it (BotQ).

The results aren't just paper promises either. Figure ran an 11-month pilot at BMW's Spartanburg plant, loading 90,000+ sheet-metal parts and supporting production of 30,000+ X3 vehicles. They demonstrated a 30-hour fully autonomous warehouse shift, moving 38,000+ packages with zero human intervention. BotQ, the manufacturing arm, has scaled from a handful of units a month in 2025 to roughly 240 a month by April 2026, on a stated path toward 12,000 units a year.

That's a real company doing real things. It's also a company that's pre-revenue at a $39 billion valuation, betting that Helix keeps improving and BotQ keeps scaling on schedule. The upside is enormous if it works. The downside is that "if it works" is doing a lot of load-bearing in that sentence.

## The Robots Are Already on the Floor: Tesla Optimus

Tesla's pitch is different. Instead of building robotics manufacturing capacity from scratch, Optimus reuses Tesla's existing EV production lines and its FSD-derived AI stack. The Fremont factory, previously making Model S and Model X, is being converted to build Optimus units at scale, with a stated target of a 1 million unit annual run rate by late 2026 and a dedicated Giga Texas facility targeting 10 million units a year in 2027.

As of January 2026, Tesla has more than 1,000 Optimus Gen 3 units physically working the Fremont factory floor: battery assembly, EV pack loading, cable routing, parts handling. On the surface, that looks like the strongest real-world proof of the three companies.

Except Elon Musk said otherwise, directly, on the January 28, 2026 earnings call: those units are "primarily for learning, not productive tasks." That's a meaningful admission. A thousand robots on a factory floor sounds like commercial deployment. What Tesla is actually describing is a very expensive, very visible R&D program. Optimus 3 production is confirmed to start Summer 2026, ramping to high volume by Summer 2027, assuming the timeline holds. Tesla has already pushed the Optimus V3 reveal back multiple times in 2026, so treat that timeline as optimistic.

Tesla has the highest ceiling of the three companies if the manufacturing scale-up goes as planned. It also has the widest gap between what's being announced and what's been demonstrated.

## The Company Nobody's Talking About: Unitree

Then there's Unitree, and this is the one most people outside the robotics press haven't heard of. No headline-grabbing mega-raise. No $39 billion valuation, until this month, when the company priced its Shanghai IPO at a $9 billion valuation, raising roughly $904 million. It's the first humanoid robotics company to actually go public. The retail tranche of the offering was oversubscribed by more than 8,000 times.

Unitree's 2025 revenue rose more than fourfold to roughly $252 million, with humanoid robot sales, not their long-running quadruped line, becoming the company's largest business for the first time. They shipped over 5,500 humanoid units in 2025. Their G1 model runs $13,500 to $16,000; the R1, launched mid-2025, starts at $5,900, dramatically undercutting both Figure and Tesla on price.

And they're profitable. Adjusted net profit around $83 million (600 million yuan) in 2025. That makes Unitree the only one of the three companies with a working business model today, not a projected one. Q1 2026 adjusted profit fell 52.6% as R&D and marketing spend ramped, so the margin story is getting tighter even as revenue grows. Unitree isn't trying to out-build Figure's AI stack or Tesla's manufacturing scale. It's winning a different game: volume and cost, backed by China's manufacturing base, where Chinese makers overall captured roughly 90% of global humanoid shipment volume in 2025-2026.

## What Actually Predicts Winning

Line the three up and the pattern is obvious once you see it: funding size and valuation don't correlate with who's actually generating revenue right now. Figure has the biggest war chest and the most credible AI story, and it's pre-revenue. Tesla has robots physically on a factory floor, and its own CEO says they're not doing productive work yet. Unitree has neither the funding headlines nor the AI-capability story, and it's the only one of the three with a profitable, shipping business.

None of that means Figure or Tesla are bad bets. Both could still win their respective segments. It means the metric everyone defaults to when sizing up a company, how much money it's raised or how big the valuation is, tells you almost nothing about whether the thing actually works today.

## The Same Test Applies to Your AI Vendor

This is where the robotics story turns into a business story. Every SMB owner evaluating an AI vendor right now is running the exact same risk of mis-reading the signal. A slick pitch deck, a big funding round, a case study slide with a logo you recognize: none of that tells you whether the automation will actually run in your business, on your data, this quarter.

The question that actually matters is the one Unitree's numbers answer and Figure's don't yet: is this thing shipping and working right now, for real customers, or is it still primarily for learning?

That's the operating principle behind how mAxIm builds. Every automation gets built, tested, and run for real before it's shown to a client. Not staged for a demo, not a roadmap slide with a "coming soon" label. If it's not live and working, it doesn't go in the pitch.

## What to Ask Before You Sign Anything

A short checklist worth running on any AI vendor pitch, robotics or otherwise:

- What's actually running today, not what's planned for next quarter?
- Can they show you the system live, on real data, not a curated demo?
- Is there a customer reference who'll tell you what actually happened, not just what was promised?
- What's the unit economics story? A company burning cash to win market share behaves very differently from one already profitable on what it ships.

Figure AI might be the company that redefines the industrial robot in five years. Tesla might hit its 2027 production numbers and flood the market with cheap humanoids. Unitree might keep winning the cost segment while everyone else chases AI capability. All three outcomes are plausible. What's not plausible is judging any of them, or any AI vendor pitching your business today, by the size of the number on their funding slide.
